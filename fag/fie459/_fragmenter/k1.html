1 · Sustainable finance and ESG issues

Sustainable finance keeps the toolkit of ordinary finance but widens what counts as value and lengthens the horizon. This chapter gives the vocabulary for the whole course: how CSR, SRI and ESG relate, what double materiality means, why investors want ESG (value or values), and the Schoenmaker & Schramade ladder from finance as usual to SF 3.0. Expect exam questions that ask you to place a short case in one of these boxes.

1.1 Finance in a nutshell

Sustainable finance changes the inputs to ordinary finance, not its logic, so start with the logic.

Investment decisions. A firm invests in real assets, tangible and intangible, when the net present value is positive. Shareholders own the firm, and the standard objective is to maximise their value, the present value of future dividends.

NPV = Σi=1n CFi / (1 + r)i − I     P0 = Σi=1∞ Di / (1 + r)i
CFi = cash flow in period i, I = investment, r = required return (cost of capital), P0 = share price, Di = dividend per share in period i.

Financing decisions. Investment is funded by retained earnings, debt (bank loans, bonds) or equity. In a perfect capital market, firm value does not depend on the mix (Modigliani–Miller Proposition I), and the cost of equity rises linearly with leverage to compensate shareholders for the added risk (Proposition II). In reality, taxes, bankruptcy costs and information asymmetry make financing matter. Those frictions are the opening for sustainability: if ESG changes a firm's risk or what investors know about it, it can change the cost of capital (chapter 9).

Risk and return. Higher expected returns usually come only with greater risk. The CAPM, E(Ri) = Rf + βi[E(Rm) − Rf], prices systematic risk, and obvious bargains are rare: no free lunch. Meet any claim that ESG is a free source of extra return with the same suspicion.

The principal–agent problem. Managers (agents) act for investors (principals), and their priorities can differ. Information asymmetry is the core. Adverse selection is taking advantage of undisclosed information in a contract; moral hazard is one party taking on extra risk that falls on the other. Remedies include corporate governance, disclosure requirements, regulation, auditing and ratings, among them ESG ratings (chapters 5 and 7).

1.2 What sustainable finance is

  • Schoenmaker & Schramade (2019): sustainable finance «looks at how Finance (investing and lending) interacts with economic, social, and environmental issues».
  • European Commission: taking environmental, social and governance (ESG) considerations into account when making investment decisions, «leading to more long-term investments in sustainable economic activities and projects».

The first is broad and runs both ways: finance affects society and nature, and they affect finance. The second is a process (ESG in the decision) with an intended result (long-term, sustainable investment).

ESG issues reach a valuation through the usual lines: revenues, costs, depreciation and taxation. The course's tools for getting them in are externalities (chapter 3), intangibles, business models, stakeholder analysis, materiality (chapter 6), scenario and sensitivity analysis, and real options. For banks, asset managers and insurers, ESG shapes asset classes, instruments, risks, opportunities and investment strategies (chapters 8–12).

Traditional financeSustainable finance
Efficient resource and capital allocationAllocation for long-term sustainability
Diversification and risk sharingSometimes less diversification for good reasons, e.g. avoiding «sin stocks»
Shareholder value maximisationStakeholder welfare maximisation

1.3 CSR, SRI and ESG: the concept chain

Starks (2023) notes there is no clear consensus on what these acronyms mean. The lecture fixes their relation in a chain:

From sustainability to measurement Sustainability requires that the interests of the firm's stakeholders are taken care of → stakeholder management → put into practice through CSR (corporate social responsibility, the corporate side) and SRI (socially responsible investing, the investor side) → the outcomes, stakeholder welfare, are what ESG metrics try to measure.
ESG is not something you do CSR and SRI are actions; ESG is measurement. A firm cutting emissions does CSR, a fund excluding coal does SRI, and a rating agency's score of either is ESG. Most empirical difficulties arise at the measurement step (chapter 7): different raters can score the same firm very differently.

1.4 Why now: the drivers

  • Science. Planetary boundaries (Steffen et al. 2015) and doughnut economics (Raworth 2017) frame the economy as operating within environmental limits (general background, not from the lecture).
  • Global agreements. The Sustainable Development Goals and the Paris Climate Agreement (both 2015), and the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022).
  • Regulation. A significant increase in ESG regulation (chapters 7 and 8).
  • Public, employees and consumers. Increasingly aware and critical of misconduct, which makes firms realise that ESG issues are financially material.
  • Investor demand. Over USD 37.5 trillion in SRI assets globally by 2021 (UN PRI); most investment companies claim to integrate ESG.

These pressures also changed what firms report. Under double materiality, an issue can matter in two directions:

Financial materialityImpact materiality
DirectionOutside-in: how sustainability issues affect the firmInside-out: how the firm affects people and planet
Climate exampleClimate change hits the companyThe company's activities affect the climate
Primary audienceInvestorsConsumers, civil society, employees, investors

The two are linked: a firm's impact can become financially material, for example once regulation or customers make it pay for the harm. Chapters 6 and 7 build on this.

1.5 Do investors expect ESG to pay?

Giglio et al. (2025) surveyed roughly 30,000 US individual investors (Vanguard clients) about their main motive:

Main motiveShare
No reason at all to invest in ESG48%
Ethical considerations24%
Climate hedging: ESG as insurance against climate risk22%
Return expectations6%

On average, respondents expect ESG investments to underperform by around 2% a year, with large differences across investors. Demand for ESG is driven far more by values than by expected value. (Starks cites an earlier version of the survey with slightly different shares; use the lecture's.)

1.6 Value versus values (Starks 2023)

Starks argues that most confusion about ESG comes from mixing two motives:

  • Value: ESG matters because it is financially material. It sheds light on risks (environmental, reputation, human capital, litigation, regulatory, corruption, climate) and return opportunities. Value investors use it in stock selection and engage management to lower risk or raise returns.
  • Values: nonpecuniary preferences. The investor does not want to supply capital to, or profit from, firms tied to objectionable products or conduct, or wants to back firms that reflect their own, for example religious, values.

Many investors hold both. Starks reads the Vanguard survey this way: the ethical group are values investors, the return group are value investors, and the climate hedgers could be either. Her spectrum of approaches (Figure 1):

ApproachTypical motivationTypical expected return
Impact investingIntentional focus on E or S outcomesSome accept lower returns for impact; some expect risk-adjusted market returns
Socially responsible investingUsually nonpecuniary; avoid firms that conflict with one's values (negative screening)Closer to risk-adjusted market returns, some willing to accept lower
Classic ESG investingSome nonpecuniary preferences, some value motivationsSpans both neighbours: from «some accept lower» to market returns or more
Traditional investingRisk and return aloneRisk-adjusted market returns or more
Why «does ESG pay?» has no single answer Studies of ESG funds lump together funds with different objectives: some screen on religious values, others integrate ESG to manage risk. A values fund may knowingly give up return; a value fund aims to earn more. The average describes neither, so Starks argues performance cannot be judged until the motives are separated. The same split explains why ESG ratings can disappoint: most are designed for value investors and assess firms' business processes and risk management, while values investors care about the outcomes for the environment and communities.
ESG investing does not imply sacrificing return Only part of the spectrum accepts lower returns, and even impact investors are split. A value investor uses ESG precisely to improve risk and return. The mirror trap: negative screening is not proof of values. It is the main tool of values investors, but value investors can use it too (Starks 2023).

1.7 The Schoenmaker & Schramade framework

The course's backbone classifies finance by the value it creates and how it ranks financial (F), social (S) and environmental (E) value.

TypologyValue createdRankingOptimisationHorizon
Finance as usualShareholder valueFMax FShort term
SF 1.0Refined shareholder valueF » S and EMax F subject to S and EShort term
SF 2.0Stakeholder value (triple bottom line)I = F + S + EOptimise IMedium term
SF 3.0Common good valueS and E > FOptimise S and E subject to FLong term

Read it top to bottom as a shift in the objective. SF 1.0 still maximises F; S and E enter only as minor constraints, such as excluding the worst offenders. SF 2.0 optimises integrated value I, so a unit of S or E counts like a unit of F, and a project with lower profit can win if it creates more S and E. SF 3.0 reverses the ranking: it optimises S and E, subject to earning enough F. The horizon lengthens from short term (finance as usual and SF 1.0) to long term (SF 3.0).

Case: place the strategy

A fund maximises return but excludes tobacco and weapons makers: SF 1.0. A utility picks the cleaner of two plants although it earns less, because F + S + E is higher: SF 2.0. An investor funds clean-water projects for their social effect and requires only that capital is preserved: SF 3.0.

Where the table returns Chapter 5 discusses the challenges of SF 1.0 and SF 2.0, chapter 8 contrasts traditional (SF 1.0) and long-term value creation (SF 2.0) investing, and chapter 9 maps portfolio objectives onto it: exclusionary screening is SF 1.0, ESG integration SF 2.0, impact investing SF 3.0.
What the exam is likely to ask Label the parts of a case as CSR, SRI or ESG; classify an investor's motive as value or values; say which direction of materiality a disclosure covers; and place an objective in the S&S table. A likely trap is SF 1.0: it is still profit maximisation, only with constraints.
