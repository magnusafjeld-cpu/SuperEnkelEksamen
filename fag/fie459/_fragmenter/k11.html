11 · Sustainability in banking

A bank's sustainability footprint sits mostly in other firms: the clients it lends to. This chapter starts with what banks do and how they earn money, shows why their material ESG issues are indirect and then builds on the lecture's main framework: risk-based versus value-based banking. It ends with sustainable loan products, the industry principles, EU bank regulation and the DNB guest lecture. Expect questions that place a bank or loan on the SF 1.0 to 3.0 ladder.

11.1 What banks do and how they earn money

Banks finance the economy and let businesses grow. They matter most for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs). Private credit by banks rivals the stock market in size (World Bank WDI): about 96% of GDP worldwide against a market capitalisation of 106%. It is larger in Norway (94% against 72%) and China (186% against 64%), smaller in the US (52% against 157%).

A bank's core skill is handling asymmetric information. It screens projects before lending to prevent adverse selection and monitors borrowers afterwards to prevent moral hazard (opportunistic behaviour during the project). It audits borrowers who fail to pay (costly state verification). It can also analyse sector-wide sustainability trends and spot best practice.

A typical bank's balance sheet is mostly loans and trading assets, funded by deposits, trading liabilities and debt, with a thin layer of equity: long-term loans funded by short-term deposits. The P&L is interest income plus fee income (underwriting, securities transactions), minus interest paid, loan losses and expenses. Profit comes mainly from the spread between lending and deposit rates. The main risks are macroeconomic and market risk, liquidity risk (depositors withdraw), operational, reputational and political risk. Above all there is credit risk, which is «indirectly all risks a counterparty faces».

11.2 Why a bank's material issues are indirect

The SASB materiality map for commercial banks lists data security, access and affordability, product design and lifecycle management, business ethics and systemic risk management. Not one environmental issue appears. The reason is that a bank's big ESG issues sit in its credits and investments: the lecture calls them the bank's indirect material issues. Schoenmaker & Schramade (2019) contrast the two kinds of firm:

The social side counts too. Cramer finds that a 2005 Reserve Bank of India policy gave treatment districts 27 more bank branches after five years; household employment and access to savings accounts rose, hospitals used more credit. Six years after the policy, households were 19 percentage points less likely to suffer non-chronic illness in a given month. Østergaard, Schindele & Vale find that Norwegian savings banks, governed by their stakeholders, were more likely to survive after deregulation in communities with high social capital, while commercial banks showed no such effect.

11.3 Risk-based versus value-based banking

This is the lecture's main framework. Schoenmaker & Schramade (2019) apply the ladder of chapter 1 to bank loans:

TypologyBank loansApproach
SF 1.0ExclusionRisk-based
SF 2.0ESG integrationRisk-based
SF 3.0Impact lending, microfinanceValue-based

In risk-based banking, sustainability criteria enter credit risk management and show up in the risk premium: a borrower with poor ESG management pays more, or is excluded. In value-based banking, the bank lends to create social and environmental value and needs only enough financial return to stay viable.

How ESG reaches a credit decision ESG factors change the borrower's creditworthiness, which moves the credit risk indicators the bank prices on. They also change the collateral value, for example of real estate. Schoenmaker & Schramade propose a four-step sustainability credit score: (1) analyse the company's industry; (2) sketch paths for the average company (business as usual, sustainable business, future sustainable business); (3) determine the material ESG issues, with the firm's vulnerabilities and opportunities; (4) weight them and combine them into an overall credit assessment. Weber (2010) finds that sustainability criteria help predict a debtor's financial performance and improve the predictive validity of the credit rating.

Does value-based banking cost return? Data from the Global Alliance for Banking on Values (GABV 2016), cited in Schoenmaker & Schramade (2019), compare values-based banks (VBBs) with global systemically important banks:

VBBs lend more of their balance sheet to the real economy and fund it with deposits. Their ROE is similar, with lower leverage, a higher ROA and much lower volatility: they are more stable.

11.4 Sustainable loan products

  • Green loans: the proceeds are tracked and allocated to green projects.
  • Sustainability-linked loans (SLLs): the interest rate is linked to the improvement of the company's ESG score or of sustainability KPIs. The money is not earmarked.

Both follow market principles from the loan market associations LMA, LSTA and APLMA: the Green Loan Principles, the Sustainability-Linked Loan Principles and, for social purposes, the Social Loan Principles. They are the loan counterparts of the green and sustainability-linked bonds of chapter 10. The UN PRI chart on the slides shows ESG loans growing fast from 2016 to 2021, with ESG-linked (sustainability-linked) loans as the larger part. Green loans go mostly to utilities; SLLs are spread across many sectors, including financials, oil and petroleum, chemicals and food.

Trap: the label tells you what is tied, not how green the borrower is A green loan ties the use of proceeds; its price need not depend on the borrower's performance. An SLL ties the price to performance targets; the money can fund anything. «An SLL must finance green projects» and «a green loan's margin follows the borrower's ESG score» are both false. A green loan can go to a polluting firm for one clean project; an SLL can go to an oil company with credible targets.

11.5 Principles for banks

The Equator Principles are «a financial industry benchmark for determining, assessing and managing environmental and social risk in projects». As of March 2021, 116 financial institutions in 37 countries had adopted them. Their ten principles cover the project cycle, from review and categorisation and an environmental and social assessment to covenants, independent monitoring and public reporting.

The UN Principles for Responsible Banking (UNEP FI, 2019) are six: alignment (with the SDGs and the Paris Agreement), impact and target setting, clients and customers, stakeholders, governance and culture, transparency and accountability. Signatories follow three steps: impact analysis, target setting and reporting. The slide, a 2021 screenshot of the UNEP FI site, shows 247 signatories (132 founding) holding 40% of global banking assets.

11.6 Regulation and the reality check

EU sustainable finance regulation rests on four pillars (Sia Partners): the taxonomy, SFDR, CSRD and the EBA ITS on Pillar 3, the prudential ESG disclosures of listed banks. Pillar 3 asks for:

  • Risk disclosures. Transition risk: exposures to fossil fuel companies and real estate by the collateral's energy performance. Physical risk: exposures to chronic and acute climate events, by sector and geography.
  • Mitigating actions that support clients' transition or adaptation without meeting taxonomy criteria, such as renovation loans or flood barriers.
  • The green asset ratio (GAR): the share of exposures to large (NFRD) corporates and retail that finances taxonomy-aligned activities. The banking book taxonomy alignment ratio (BTAR) extends this to non-NFRD corporates.
  • Qualitative disclosures on governance, risk management, business model and strategy.

The lecture ends with a reality check from the Financial Times: banks boosted fossil fuel finance by more than a fifth in 2024; US banks and Barclays each increased it by more than USD 10 billion.

In its ESG risk assessment, DNB judges a client's inherent ESG risks and how well it mitigates them along four subjects: goal achievement (delivery on ESG targets against peers), action plans (and the capacity to deliver them), responsible business conduct (controversies, ESG reporting) and compliance (with current law and readiness for upcoming regulation). Why should banks care? To attract talent and capital, for new growth opportunities, for risk mitigation and for their license to operate.

The speakers also introduced avoided emissions (WBCSD): the estimated difference in full life-cycle emissions between a scenario where a solution is used and a reference scenario, the most likely alternative without it. They traced sustainable finance through five phases on a hype curve: exclusion (1987–2001), ethics and active ownership (2002–2014), green competitiveness (2015–2019), regulation (2020–2024, when ESG requirements reached SMEs through banks' credit process) and «End of ESG?» from 2025, after Northvolt's bankruptcy filing and the suspension of Net Zero Asset Managers. The open question is a healthy recalibration: What is financially material? What is our transition plan?

The dilemmas have no clean answer: renewables against biodiversity and land, democracy against pace, security of supply against lower emissions, local mineral extraction against imports from countries without human rights protection. The cases were protests against DNB over the Dakota Access Pipeline and the 2023 Oslo protests over the Fosen wind farm (general background, not from the lecture: Norway's Supreme Court ruled in 2021 that the Fosen licences violated Sami reindeer herders' rights). How far does a bank's responsibility go? Should it hold clients to higher standards than the law?

What the exam is likely to ask Place a bank activity on the ladder: exclusion and ESG integration are risk-based (SF 1.0 and 2.0), impact lending and microfinance value-based (SF 3.0). Tell a green loan (use of proceeds) from an SLL (price tied to KPIs).
