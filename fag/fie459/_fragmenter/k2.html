2 · Business ethics

Before a firm or investor can act responsibly, someone has to see a problem as a moral one and decide what is right. Business ethics offers two kinds of theory: normative theories say what is right, descriptive theories explain how people actually decide. The exam typically quotes a short argument or case and asks which theory, stage, factor or tactic it shows.

2.1 What business ethics is

Business ethics is «the rules, principles, and standards of deciding what is morally right or wrong when working» (Cambridge Dictionary). The lecture opens with a case: Elon Musk reopening a Tesla factory in 2020 in violation of state orders. Three questions run through the chapter: was the decision morally right (normative theories), what influenced it (descriptive theories), and how should a shareholder react (chapters 4 and 5)?

2.2 Normative theories: what is right?

The first split is whether morality is universal. Ethical absolutism holds that there are eternal, universally applicable moral principles. Ethical relativism holds that morality is context-dependent and subjective. Absolutist theories split again by what they judge:

TypeTheoryFocusGuiding tenet
Consequentialist (outcomes)Ethical egoismIndividual desires or interestsMaximise one's own self-interest
Consequentialist (outcomes)UtilitarianismOutcomes and collective welfareBest total outcome for all affected; act or rule utilitarianism
Non-consequentialist (principles)Ethics of dutyDutiesKant's categorical imperative: act only on principles that could become universal law
Non-consequentialist (principles)Rights and justiceRights and the nature of justiceUniversalisable rules of justice; respect for human dignity

Act utilitarianism judges each action by its consequences; rule utilitarianism follows the rules that produce the best consequences in general (general background, not from the lecture).

Outcomes or principles: one act, two verdicts Consequentialist theories judge an act by its results; non-consequentialist theories judge it by the principle behind it. So one act can pass one test and fail the other. A utilitarian judges Tesla's reopening by adding up the benefits (output, jobs, the firm's survival) and the harms the orders were meant to prevent. A Kantian asks whether the rule «a firm may ignore orders it disagrees with» could hold for every firm. It could not, so the act is wrong whatever the outcome.

The lecture groups four contemporary theories under relativism:

TheoryFocusGuiding tenet
Virtue ethicsCharacterStrive to be a good person; learn virtuous traits to lead a good life
Ethics of careRelationshipsOur first responsibility is to our significant relationships
Discourse ethicsDeliberationResolve conflicts through honest, open exchange and argument
Postmodern ethicsMoral impulseEthics is specific to context and individual; an innate internal guide
Egoism is consequentialist Ethical egoism and utilitarianism both judge by outcomes; they differ only in whose outcomes count (one's own or everyone's). Kant's categorical imperative is not about outcomes at all. A statement that calls egoism non-consequentialist, or says Kant maximises welfare, is false.

2.3 Descriptive theories: how decisions are made

Descriptive theories explain how ethical decisions are actually made in business and what influences them. Schwartz (2016) contrasts the rationalist perspective, the common assumption of logical reasoning, like a calculation, before a judgement, with the intuitionist/sentimentalist perspective, which stresses intuition, emotion and quick moral intuitions.

Four stages, and each can fail The course's framework (Crane et al. 2019, based on Rest 1986 and Jones 1991): recognise the moral issue → make a moral judgement → establish moral intent → engage in moral behaviour. Individual and situational factors influence every stage. A person can fail at any link: not see the issue, judge correctly but not intend to act, or intend to act but not follow through. The model is a simple way to present a complex process, not a definitive account.

Case: place the reasoning

«I won't inflate the valuation; compliance would catch me» is stage 1. «I'll approve your numbers if you approve mine next quarter» is stage 2. «Everyone on the desk does it» is stage 3. «We disclose the risk because investors have a right to know, though no law requires it» is stage 5.

Personal integrity shows most clearly in whistleblowing: an employee intentionally exposing perceived ethical or legal violations by the company. It is an important way such issues are reported and policed, but it needs protection. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act requires US-listed companies to provide channels for anonymous reporting, and several countries have specific whistleblower protection laws.

2.5 Situational factors

Issue-related factors belong to the problem itself: moral intensity and moral framing. Context-related factors belong to the setting: rewards, authority, bureaucracy, work roles, organisational culture and national context. The evidence is strong for rewards, authority (superiors and top management) and organisational culture, and thinner for bureaucracy, work roles and national context.

Moral intensity (Jones 1991) varies with six factors. The one-line questions are general background, not from the lecture:

FactorQuestion it asks
Magnitude of consequencesHow much harm or benefit?
Social consensusHow widely do people agree it is wrong?
Probability of effectHow likely is the harm?
Temporal immediacyHow soon will it happen?
ProximityHow close are those affected to the decision maker?
Concentration of effectDoes the harm fall heavily on a few or thinly on many?
Why climate harm feels morally weak One firm's emissions score low on temporal immediacy (decades away), proximity (distant people) and concentration (spread thinly over millions), and their link to any specific harm is uncertain. Low intensity means the issue is less likely to be recognised as moral at all, which is the first stage of the Rest model. It is one reason externalities (chapter 3) persist even among well-meaning managers.

Moral framing. The same dilemma is perceived very differently depending on how it is framed, and moral language is more likely to trigger moral thinking: «tax optimisation» and «dodging tax» describe the same act. The opposite is moral muteness (Bird & Walters 1989): managers avoid moral talk because they see it as a threat to harmony, to efficiency, and to their image of power and effectiveness.

Rationalisation tactics redescribe an act so it no longer looks wrong:

TacticSounds like
Denial of responsibility«I had no choice; head office set the targets.»
Denial of injury«No customer actually lost money.»
Denial of victim«They knew the risks when they signed.»
Social weighting«Who are you to judge? Our rivals are far worse.»
Appeal to higher loyalties«I did it to protect the team.»
Metaphor of the ledger«After all my unpaid overtime, I've earned this.»
Individual or situational? Cognitive moral development, locus of control, personal values and integrity are individual factors. Moral intensity and moral framing are situational (issue-related); rewards, authority and culture are situational (context-related). An item that lists moral intensity among the individual factors is false.

2.6 From ethics to economics: three views of CSR

Bénabou and Tirole (2010) ask who wants the CSR and whose money pays for it:

ViewWhose values drive itWhat it is
Win-win: doing well by doing goodNone needed; CSR pays for itself through reputation, employee retention and lower riskLong-run value maximisation, no ethical tension
Delegated philanthropyStakeholders' prosocial preferences (customers, employees, shareholders)The firm as an agent for others' values
Insider-initiated corporate philanthropyManagement's own valuesSpending other people's money: an agency problem, not an ethical achievement

The same donation can be any of the three: win-win if it lifts the brand, delegated if customers and shareholders want it, insider-initiated if only the CEO wants it and shareholders pay. Win-win matches the value motive from chapter 1, delegated philanthropy the values motive. The agency view returns in chapters 4 and 5.

2.7 Case: Telenor in Myanmar

WhenWhat happened
2013–2014Telenor launches and expands fast. In 2012 fewer than one in ten people in Myanmar had a mobile phone; by 2021 the whole population had access.
Feb 2021Military coup, surveillance pressures, ethical conflict. Telenor's mission on entering in 2014 had been «to help build society».
Aug 2021Telenor sells: it will not be possible to operate according to its values and international laws and standards.
Aug 2021Criticised for «irresponsible disengagement»: accused of sharing sensitive user data with the junta after the coup, and of selling to a company closely linked to the junta.
LaterTelenor publishes learning outcomes from its Myanmar engagement.
Case: stay or leave?

A utilitarian compares total welfare: staying keeps a network many people rely on but may mean serving surveillance; leaving ends Telenor's own role but hands the network, and its user data, to the junta's side. A rights and justice view centres on users' privacy and safety, which both options may violate. Ethics of care asks what Telenor owes local employees and customers. On moral intensity, the harm is large, immediate and concentrated on identifiable users, so it is hard to overlook. The critics' point: exit is not automatically responsible if the buyer continues the harm.

Exit that moves the harm Selling a problem to a less scrutinised owner returns in chapter 3 (public firms selling polluting assets to less-monitored private firms) and in chapter 5 (exit versus voice).
What the exam is likely to ask Classify a quoted argument (egoism, utilitarianism, duty, rights, virtue, care, discourse, postmodern); order the four Rest/Jones stages; place a statement on Kohlberg's ladder; name the moral intensity factor or rationalisation tactic in a quote; decide whether a factor is individual or situational; and say which Bénabou–Tirole view a CSR decision reflects.
