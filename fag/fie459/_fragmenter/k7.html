7 · Metrics and reporting: who is the report for?

Reporting turns the analysis of chapter 6 into public information, and every framework first answers one question: who is the reader? Investors want to know how sustainability affects the firm; other stakeholders want to know how the firm affects the world. The EU's CSRD asks for both. The exam tests which framework does what, the three tests of the EU Taxonomy, why ESG ratings disagree, and the PwC guest lecture's message that reporting is only worth it when it feeds strategy.

7.1 The voluntary frameworks

Companies report under a mix of voluntary standards and mandatory rules. The voluntary frameworks differ mainly in their reader and their lens:

FrameworkCoversLens and reader
GRI (Global Reporting Initiative)Impacts on the economy, environment and people, topic by topic (e.g. GRI 305 Emissions, 2016)Impact materiality; all stakeholders
Integrated Reporting (<IR>)How the firm creates value using six capitals, in one reportLinks financial and sustainability information
TCFDClimate-related risks and opportunities and their governance, disclosed in annual financial filingsFinancial materiality, "consistent with how companies determine the materiality of other information"; investors and companies
CDP (Carbon Disclosure Project)GHG emissions, water, forests and supply chain, via CDP's online platform; aligned with TCFDInvestors, companies, cities, states and regions
ISSB (IFRS)Investor-focused sustainability standards, aligned with TCFDFinancial materiality; investors
One question sorts the frameworks Ask who the report is for. If it is investors, the framework asks how sustainability issues affect the firm's cash flows and risk (outside-in, financial materiality): SASB, ISSB and TCFD. If it is all stakeholders, it asks how the firm affects people and planet (inside-out, impact materiality): GRI. CSRD in the EU requires both lenses at once, which is double materiality from chapter 6.

7.2 Integrated Reporting and the six capitals

<IR> widens the view from the two capitals firms usually report on, financial and manufactured, to six.

CapitalWhat it is
FinancialFunds available to the firm: debt, equity, grants, retained earnings
ManufacturedPhysical objects used in production: buildings, equipment, infrastructure
IntellectualKnowledge-based intangibles: patents, copyrights, software, systems, procedures
HumanPeople's competencies, capabilities, experience and motivation to innovate
Social and relationshipRelationships within and between communities and stakeholders
NaturalRenewable and non-renewable environmental resources and processes

The evidence in the lecture sorts firms into four groups: no ESG reporting, stand-alone ESG reporting, ESG reporting in the annual report, and <IR>. <IR> beats no ESG reporting and ESG reporting in the annual report, but firms gain nothing by switching from a stand-alone ESG report to <IR>. One reading: a dedicated ESG report already puts the information in front of users, so repackaging it adds little.

7.3 TCFD and CDP

The Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD, 2017) asks for disclosure in four pillars:

PillarWhat the firm discloses
GovernanceThe board's oversight of climate-related risks and opportunities, and management's role
StrategyThe actual and potential impact of these risks and opportunities on the business, strategy and financial planning over the short, medium and long term, and the strategy's resilience under climate scenarios, including a 2°C or lower scenario
Risk managementHow climate risks are identified, assessed and managed, and how this fits overall risk management
Metrics and targetsThe metrics used, Scope 1, Scope 2 and, if appropriate, Scope 3 emissions, and targets with performance against them

Its seven principles for effective disclosure say disclosures should be relevant; specific and complete; clear, balanced and understandable; consistent over time; comparable within a sector, industry or portfolio; reliable, verifiable and objective; and timely. CDP collects carbon, water and forest data through an online platform and, unlike TCFD, also covers cities, states and regions.

7.4 Mandatory reporting in the EU

The EU has three instruments: the EU Taxonomy; the Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD, about 12,000 companies), replaced by the Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD, about 50,000); and the SFDR for asset managers, pension funds and insurers (chapter 8).

The Taxonomy defines which economic activities count as environmentally sustainable. Its goals include meeting the EU's 2030 climate and energy targets, protecting investors from greenwashing, and shifting investment to where it is most needed. An activity is taxonomy-aligned only if it passes all three tests, measured by technical screening criteria per activity and objective:

  1. Substantial contribution to at least one of six environmental objectives: climate change mitigation, climate change adaptation, water and marine resources, circular economy, pollution prevention and control, biodiversity and ecosystems.
  2. Do no significant harm (DNSH) to any of the other five.
  3. Minimum safeguards: the OECD Guidelines for Multinational Enterprises and the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights.
Illustration: a wind farm on a wetland

A utility builds a wind farm, a substantial contribution to climate change mitigation, and meets the minimum safeguards. But construction drains a protected wetland, significant harm to biodiversity and ecosystems. It fails test 2, so the activity is not taxonomy-aligned. One strong contribution cannot buy off harm elsewhere.

CSRD companies report under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS), based on double materiality and on their IROs: impacts, risks and opportunities. PwC showed the resulting structure: general disclosures (ESRS 2), then environmental (e.g. E1 Climate change), social (e.g. S1 Own workforce) and governance (G1 Business conduct) standards, plus the Taxonomy disclosures. The management report moves from vague lines such as "We do not pollute the external environment more than the norm in our industry" to a standardised structure based on mandatory standards.

On 26 February 2025 the Commission adopted the Omnibus simplification package, which PwC described as a drastic change in scope:

Pre-OmnibusWho reports
Wave 1Large public-interest listed companies, banks, insurers and credit institutions with at least 500 employees and turnover above EUR 50m or balance sheet above EUR 25m
Wave 2Companies meeting two of three: 250 employees, turnover above EUR 50m, balance sheet above EUR 25m
Wave 3Listed SMEs meeting two of three: 10–250 employees, turnover EUR 10–50m, balance sheet EUR 5–25m
Post-Omnibus
From financial year 2027At least 1,000 employees and net turnover above EUR 450m. The Commission estimates that 80% of the companies previously covered fall outside.

7.5 Does reporting work?

A review of greenwashing research in the lecture finds that an entirely voluntary CSR approach facilitates greenwashing, and proposes a combination of voluntary and mandatory elements to prevent it. Krueger, Sautner, Tang and Zhong (2021) study the effects of mandatory ESG disclosure.

The HBR article "Overselling Sustainability Reporting" lists the problems. For reporting: no mandates or auditing, specious targets, opaque supply chains, complexity, confusing information, and inattention to developing countries. For sustainable investing: unhelpful definitions of "sustainable", unreliable ratings, lack of comparability, and difficulty judging and scaling real impact. Its remedy starts with "measure less, better": fewer frameworks, with reports mandated and audited by an empowered referee.

7.6 ESG ratings

The lecture follows ESG data from companies to data providers and investors, with MSCI's rating calculation as an example. Kotsantonis (2019) lists what goes wrong:

  • Companies report in varied and inconsistent ways.
  • Benchmarking, the choice of peers, largely decides a company's ranking.
  • ESG data providers disagree a lot, and the disagreement increases with the quantity of publicly available information.

A critique quoted in the lecture sums it up: the ratings "are too subjective, and the industry overpromises and underdelivers". New developments include open-data initiatives such as OS-Climate and the Terravera Foundation.

Traps for true/false "More disclosure makes ESG ratings converge" is false: disagreement increases with more public information, since raters have more to interpret differently. "A taxonomy-aligned activity must contribute to all six objectives" is false: at least one, with no significant harm to the rest. "Voluntary reporting is the best defence against greenwashing" is false: purely voluntary reporting facilitates it.

7.7 PwC guest lecture: from reporting to steering

The trust gap. 94% of investors think corporate reporting contains unsupported sustainability claims, i.e. greenwashing (PwC Global Investor Survey 2023). CSRD's mandatory, assured reporting is meant to close it.

"It's really not about the reporting." PwC treats three approaches as equally important: reporting- and compliance-driven, strategy-driven and transformation-driven. The work must run all the way from strategy to reporting. Sustainability should be part of the core strategy, not a separate sustainability strategy beside it, and "fluffy" targets no longer stand: a target needs a base year and a long-term target state. CSRD also makes the requirements for a climate transition plan more explicit.

"You can't manage what you don't measure" (the slide credits Peter Drucker). Data move up a ladder: reporting (what has happened?), monitoring (what happens right now?) and steering (what will happen?), from regulatory compliance through operational decisions to strategic decisions. PwC's reporting survey links the value firms get from reporting to using it to inform other business operations.

Climate risk is a today issue. Global losses from natural disasters were estimated at USD 417 billion in 2024. Physical impacts will worsen exponentially rather than linearly, hitting both the balance sheet (assets) and the P&L (supply chains). A firm's carbon footprint is irrelevant to its exposure: physical risk arrives through own operations, supply chains and customer markets. Myths to bust include "it is ages away, 2050", "it will happen somewhere else" and "the financial impact will be immaterial".

First years of CSRD. The first 250 reports show material topics differing across sectors, so the materiality assessment made reports relevant for their sector. About 30% of the CSRD reports were voluntary, from countries that had not transposed the directive or had delayed it. The Norwegian cost estimates (NOU 2023:15) point to significant costs. Data quality is the practical weak spot: unclear indicator definitions, little standardisation across borders, many contributors, fragmented systems, manual spreadsheets and weak documentation. PwC's advice: keep financial and sustainability reporting consistent from the start, focus on material matters, use digital tools critically and build multidisciplinary teams. The goal is a just transition, "not the sustainability statement in itself".
