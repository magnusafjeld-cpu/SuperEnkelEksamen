3 · Externalities: when prices leave out the damage

An externality is a cost or benefit that lands on someone outside the deal, so the price ignores it and the market makes the wrong amount. This chapter covers why, how externalities get internalised and what blocks it, and how to measure the effect on a firm. The exam tests classifications, the direction of the distortion, the limitations and the logic of the cases.

3.1 What an externality is

An externality is a positive or negative effect on agents who are not involved in the production or consumption of the product or service. In a free-market economy, prices are set by supply and demand without government intervention. When those prices leave out an effect, the result is a market failure: the free market allocates resources at a level that is not socially optimal, and social welfare is lost.

Example: two firms and toxic wastewater

Firms A and B each choose a technology. Option 1: revenue 100, cost 20, and a cost of 10 to the other firm (toxic water waste). Option 2: revenue 100, cost 25, and no cost to anyone else.

Private choice. Option 1 profit = 100 − 20 = 80. Option 2 profit = 100 − 25 = 75. Both firms choose option 1, because the 10 lands on someone else.

Social welfare. Option 1: (100 − 20 − 10) × 2 = 140. Option 2: (100 − 25) × 2 = 150. Society loses 150 − 140 = 10.

Why. Polluting saves each firm 5 but costs the other 10. With both on option 1, each ends up with 100 − 20 − 10 = 70, less than 75. Make firm A pay the 10 it imposes on B, and option 1 earns it 70, so it switches: internalisation in one line.

3.2 Why the market makes too much

In the lecture's diagram, supply is the marginal production cost (MPC) and demand is the marginal production benefit, here equal to the marginal societal benefit. With a negative production externality, the marginal societal cost (MSC) curve lies above supply. The market settles where demand meets MPC, at quantity Qe and price Pe. The social optimum is where demand meets MSC, at Qso and Pso.

Why the free market overproduces The producer prices only its own cost. The gap between MSC and MPC, the external cost per unit, appears in nobody's price. So the good is too cheap (Pe < Pso) and buyers take too much of it (Qe > Qso). Every unit between Qso and Qe costs society more than it is worth to the buyer.

By the same logic, a positive externality leads the market to make too little, because nobody is paid for the benefit to others (general background, not from the lecture, which draws only the negative case).

Trap: which way the price is wrong A negative production externality makes the market price too low and the quantity too high. The polluter's price is low precisely because someone else pays part of the cost. And an externality falls on a party outside the transaction: a cost that the firm or its own customers bear is not one.

3.3 The root cause: effects the product does not carry

The neoclassical production function Q = F(K, L) turns capital and labour into output. The enlarged production function, based on ecological economics, adds what that leaves out:

q + w = F(K, L, N, S, H; r, e)
q = output and w = waste (flows out); K = financial capital, L = labour, N = natural capital, S = social capital, H = human capital (funds used); r and e = flows of natural resources and energy.

Waste and the use of natural, social and human capital are not physically embodied in the product, so they do not show up in its price. That is the root cause of externalities.

Case: Velsicol

The lecture's discussion case: the chemical manufacturer Velsicol, sued for knowingly polluting water with cancer-causing pesticides. A negative production externality, since the harm falls on people who neither make nor buy the product. The lawsuit internalises it by turning the damage into a cost for the firm.

3.4 Internalisation: making the polluter pay

Internalisation is the process of bringing the costs or benefits of external effects into the decisions and prices of the parties involved, so that those responsible bear the costs, or enjoy the benefits, of their actions. Five groups can push it:

  • Government: taxes, quotas, subsidies, awareness campaigns.
  • Civil society: media attention.
  • Investors: externalities in investment and lending strategy, engagement with companies.
  • Corporates: the cost of externalities built into business practices.
  • Consumers: buying sustainable products and services.

Competition can force it too: innovation by competitors (the lecture's illustration is Tesla) can make a firm's externalities a commercial problem before any law does.

Internalisation is a spectrum, not a switch (Unerman, Bebbington & O'Dwyer 2018): an unrecognised externality; risks and opportunities recognised as values and science change; voluntary initiatives (mitigation costs, but also cost savings and new income); mandatory internalisation of part of the impact (taxes, fines); and full internalisation through a legislative ban.

3.5 Why internalisation stalls

If EU climate policy (the EU Emissions Trading System) tightens and you run plants in the EU, one answer is to relocate or outsource. That is the first of three limitations:

Territoriality of jurisdictionFirms shift production to regions with weaker regulationAfter California adopted cap-and-trade in 2013, firms shifted production and GHG emissions to other states (Bartram, Hou & Kim 2022)
Information asymmetry: greenwashingOutsiders cannot verify how green a firm isThe UK Advertising Standards Authority banned Shell adverts shown in 2022 that left out its more polluting fossil-fuel work. Political frictionsPolicy uncertainty and lobbyingFirms spend on average USD 277k a year on anti-climate lobbying and USD 185k on pro-climate lobbying (Leippold, Sautner & Yu 2024)
Trap: a greener firm, the same planet When a public firm sells a polluting plant, its own footprint shrinks, but the plant keeps running under a less monitored private owner. Relocation works the same way: emissions move rather than fall. A statement that equates a greener firm with lower total emissions is usually false.

3.6 Measuring the effect: integrated value

Schoenmaker & Schramade measure a production process by its integrated value, I = F + S + E: the sum of financial, social and environmental value. External costs enter S and E as negative numbers. Optimising I is the SF 2.0 objective (section 1.7).

Example: from financial value to integrated value (S&S Figure 2.3)
ProcessFSEI
Original15−3−39
Optimised14−1−112

Original: I = 15 − 3 − 3 = 9. Optimised: I* = 14 − 1 − 1 = 12. Switching lowers financial value by 15 − 14 = 1 and raises integrated value by 12 − 9 = 3.

Why a firm would give up financial value Read I as what the firm would be worth if every externality were charged to it. Once government (taxation, regulation), employees (strikes, talent drain) or the public (reputation, customer strikes) make it pay, the old process becomes obsolete and its assets become stranded assets that lose financial value. To avoid that, a firm and its financiers may internalise before others force them to.

3.7 Uncertainty: scenarios and stress tests

Nobody knows when, or how hard, an externality will be internalised. The lecture names three sources of uncertainty: technology, policy, and consumer behaviour and preferences. Two tools handle them:

  • Scenario analysis: determine the most important uncertainties, elaborate scenarios (developments, trends, possible external actions), present them, and weigh the probability of each.
  • Stress testing: ask "what if everything goes wrong?", that is, how the company would fare in a drastic scenario.

Scenario analysis weighs several outcomes; a stress test looks only at the severe one. For climate, the lecture uses the scenarios of the Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS).

3.8 Case: Air France-KLM and a carbon price

An airline's large negative externality is its environmental impact. The case (Schoenmaker & Schramade, with the airline's 2017 annual report) makes it visible by putting a price on it.

Case: what a carbon charge would do to Air France-KLM

Data (2017). Scope 1 and 2 CO₂ emissions: 32.7 million tonnes. Sales: EUR 25,784 million. EBIT: EUR 1,423 million. Net income: EUR −274 million.

Step 1: the charge. Apply a carbon price to the emissions, whether or not such a tax is in place: CO₂ charge = carbon price × emissions. At EUR 100 per tonne, in EUR million: 32.7 × 100 = 3,270, about EUR 3.3 billion. That is 3,270 / 1,423 = 2.30 times EBIT, or 3,270 / 25,784 = 12.7% of sales against an EBIT margin of 1,423 / 25,784 = 5.5%.

Step 2: pass-on. The EBIT hit depends on how much of the charge the airline can pass on to customers through higher fares: EBIT hit = charge × (1 − pass-on rate). At 0% pass-on the hit is the full charge; at 100% it is zero. In EUR billion, with EBIT of 1.42 before the charge:

Reading it. At EUR 100/t the airline keeps a positive EBIT only if it passes on more than 1 − 1,423 / 3,270 = 56.5% of the charge. With no pass-on, EBIT is gone from a carbon price of 1,423 / 32.7 = 43.5 euros per tonne. Pass-on is likely low in a price-competitive industry, and net income was already negative. Peer data, which might show how rivals fare, are incomplete and hard to compare.

What the exam is likely to ask Classify an externality in the 2×2; say which way price and quantity are wrong; match a case to one of the three limitations; reason with integrated value and pass-on. Know directions, not arithmetic: the charge scales with carbon price × emissions, pass-on shrinks the EBIT hit, and early internalisation protects against stranded assets.
